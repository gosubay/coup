Game Log

How to play Coup

You are the head of a family in an Italian city-state, a city run by a corrupt court. You have two influences — face-down character cards — and a pile of coins. Everyone lies about what they hold. Lose both influences and you are out. The last player with influence wins.

The five characters

The deck holds three copies of each character — 15 cards. Each player is dealt 2; the rest form the face-down Court Deck. Copies you have seen (revealed dead cards, cards in your own hand) are the raw material of every read you make.

Turn structure

Play proceeds clockwise. On your turn you take exactly one action. You may claim any character, whether or not you hold it — that is the whole game. After you declare, other players get the chance to challenge your claim, and then to block you.

  • 1. Declare. Choose an action, and a target if the action needs one.
  • 2. Challenge window. If the action requires a character, any other living player may challenge the claim. First challenge stands.
  • 3. Block window. If the action can be blocked, the eligible player(s) may block by claiming a blocking character. A block can itself be challenged.
  • 4. Resolve. If nothing stopped it, the action happens.

Challenges

A challenge says: "you don't have that card." The claimant must prove it.

  • Claim was true. The claimant reveals the card. The challenger loses an influence. The revealed card is shuffled back into the Court Deck and the claimant draws a replacement — so a proven claim rotates you into a new, unknown card.
  • Claim was a bluff. The bluffer loses an influence and the action (or block) does not happen.

Blocks are claims too, so blocks can be challenged with exactly the same consequences.

Losing influence

When you lose an influence, you choose which of your face-down cards to reveal. It stays face up for everyone to see for the rest of the game and can no longer be used or claimed honestly. Lose both and you are eliminated; your coins leave the game with you.

Coins

  • Every player starts with 2 coins. In a two-player game the first player starts with 1.
  • At the start of your turn with 10 or more coins you must Coup — no other action is legal.
  • Stealing from a player with 1 coin takes 1. You cannot Steal from a player with 0 coins.

Rulings used by this implementation

  • Assassination coins are spent on declaration and are never refunded — not if the assassination is blocked by a Contessa, and not if the Assassin claim is successfully challenged. This is the standard tournament ruling and it is what makes a failed assassin bluff so expensive.
  • Coup coins are likewise spent; Coup cannot be blocked or challenged, so it always lands.
  • If the target of an assassination loses an influence from a failed block-challenge and the assassination then resolves, they lose a second influence — a double kill.
  • The first challenge declared stands; the challenge window closes after it.
  • Foreign Aid may be blocked by any living player claiming Duke, not just the target.
  • The treasury is unlimited.

Reading the table

The chips under each seat are the claims that player has made — DUKE means they have at some point acted as a Duke and were not caught. That is public information and it is the backbone of good play: a player who taxed twice and then blocks Foreign Aid is either a real Duke or betting their whole game on one bluff.

Winning

The last player holding at least one influence wins. In practice the game is won by the player who spends the fewest influences proving things they didn't have to prove.

GTO Strategy

Everything below is read off a heads-up counterfactual-regret solve of this exact rule set — 219,187 information sets, no human theory added. The solver plays a mixed strategy: it does not tell you what to do, it tells you how often. This page is the translation of those frequencies into something you can actually hold in your head at the table.

The whole solution in one sentence

Take the action your cards support, take Foreign Aid when they support nothing, bluff the Assassin far more than you bluff anything else, and challenge only when you have two influences and your opponent has one.

The nine laws

  1. Foreign Aid is the engine. Income is the fallback.

    Below three coins the solver takes Foreign Aid 30–38% of the time and Income 18–22%. Across every coin count it is the most common single action in the solution at 21.6% of all turns — ahead of Assassinate at 19.8% and Income at 16.0%.

    Why: Foreign Aid cannot be challenged. The only way to stop it is a Duke claim, and that claim is itself challengeable — so stopping you costs your opponent risk, while taking it costs you none. It is the only place in Coup where you get a free roll.

  2. Claim what you hold. Bluff the rest a few percent of the time.

    Given the chance, the solver claims Tax 5.2%, Steal 3.5% and Exchange 1.6% of the time without the card — against 39%, 36% and 25% with it. Because you hold any given role only about a quarter to a third of the time, that still leaves 29% of Tax claims, 17% of Steals and 15% of Exchanges as bluffs.

    Why: that ratio is exactly what makes a challenge break even. Bluff more and challenges become profitable; bluff less and you leave value on the table when you actually hold the card.

  3. The Assassin is the exception — bluff it hard.

    Without an Assassin the solver still assassinates 17.7% of the time it can afford to. Four in ten assassination attempts at equilibrium are bluffs — by far the loosest claim in the game.

    Why: an assassination forces an immediate answer. If it is not challenged and not blocked, it takes half of everything your opponent has for three coins. A caught bluff costs one influence and the coins; a successful one is usually decisive. Nothing else in the game pays like that.

  4. Challenging is a luxury of the two-influence player.

    Challenge frequency against Tax, Steal and Exchange, by influence count:

    Why: a lost challenge costs one influence. On two influences that is a bruise; on one it is the game. And a won challenge against a one-influence opponent ends the game on the spot — so the same call is worth three times as much when they are short.

  5. Count copies before you call.

    There are three of each card. Everything you can see — your own hand plus every face-up revealed card — is a copy your opponent cannot have. Challenge rate against a claim, by copies visible to you:

    Why: with all three copies accounted for, the claim is a certain bluff and the challenge is free money — the solver takes it 56–88% of the time and only misses because those positions are rare enough to stay slightly unconverged. This is the single largest, most reliable edge available to a human player, and it requires no reads at all — only counting.

  6. Blocks are bluffed about as often as they are honest.

    Honest share of each block at equilibrium: Duke blocks Foreign Aid 56%, Captain blocks Steal 57%, Contessa blocks assassination 59%, Ambassador blocks Steal 64%. A block is a much weaker signal than an action.

    Why: a block is defensive, so it is cheap to attempt — you only pay if you are challenged. Actions cost tempo and coins whether or not they are believed, which is why claims made on your own turn are 71–85% honest and blocks are not.

  7. Doubt the Contessa. Believe everything else.

    The solver challenges a Contessa block 49% of the time. It challenges a Duke block of Foreign Aid 10%, a Captain block 11%, an Ambassador block 11%.

    Why: the other three blocks defend two or three coins. The Contessa block defends an influence, and if you break it the assassination lands on top of the lost challenge — a double kill for three coins. Same claim, five times the prize.

  8. On your last influence, facing an assassin, never fold.

    With a Contessa: block 96%. Without one: bluff-block 61%, challenge 38%, pass 0.9%. On two influences with no Contessa the same spot is a fold 91% of the time.

    Why: passing is death, so every alternative is better regardless of the odds. The split between bluff-blocking and challenging is there to stop your opponent reading you — the bluff-block is the larger half because a challenge is right only when they actually lack the Assassin, while a block wins whenever it goes unchallenged.

  9. Watch the seven-coin line — theirs and yours.

    The Foreign Aid block rate sits near 25% while the opponent is poor and jumps to 42%, 47%, 49% as they reach 7, 8 and 9 coins. Steals concentrate on the opponent sitting at 7–8 coins (56% / 51%). At 7–9 coins yourself the solver coups 45–63% of the time with no Assassin, but prefers to assassinate (34–54%) when it holds one.

    Why: seven coins converts money into a guaranteed influence. Everything before that line is about reaching it first; everything after is about spending it before your opponent can take it away.

Simplifications that cost almost nothing

A human cannot run 219,187 frequencies. These six approximations reproduce most of the solver's behaviour:

On your turn

  • Hold the card? Use it — Tax, Steal or Exchange about one turn in three, mixing in Foreign Aid and Income so you are not readable.
  • Hold nothing? Foreign Aid. Take Income instead when a Duke is already visible or your opponent has been blocking.
  • Hold the Assassin and 3+ coins? Assassinate — two thirds of the time. Without one, still bluff it about one time in six.
  • 7 coins? Coup, unless you hold an Assassin — then assassinate and keep the coup in reserve. At 10, you have no choice.

On their turn

  • Two influences vs their one? Challenge about one claim in three. Otherwise challenge roughly one in ten, and on your last influence almost never.
  • Can you see two copies? Roughly double your challenge rate. Three copies — always challenge.
  • Foreign Aid? Block it a quarter of the time; block it half the time once they are at seven coins, whether or not you hold the Duke.
  • Steal? Block it half the time. The claim matters less than the fact that blocking is nearly free.
  • Assassination on your last influence? Block, or challenge. Never pass.
  • They blocked your assassination? Challenge it — this is a coin flip the solver takes 49% of the time, and it is the only block worth doubting.

The three numbers that drive everything

Strip the tables away and the entire solution is a function of three quantities. Every frequency on this page moves when one of them moves, and almost nothing else matters:

  • Copies visible. How many of the claimed card you can already account for. This sets challenge frequency, and it is pure arithmetic — no read required.
  • Whose influence is cheaper. Two-vs-one is the aggression switch: the player with the spare influence challenges three times as often and bluffs more, the player on one influence stops calling almost entirely.
  • Distance to seven coins. Coins have no value except as a coup, so the closer either player is to seven, the more the game turns into a race — blocks tighten, steals target the leader, and cheap actions stop being worth a turn.

The solver plays two-handed Coup. With more players the arithmetic of copies is unchanged, but challenges get cheaper for the table and more expensive for you — a challenge you win removes an influence that everyone shares the benefit of. Expect real multi-way play to challenge less than these numbers, not more.

Frequencies are reach-weighted averages over the solver's converged policy — each information set counted in proportion to how often equilibrium play actually reaches it. The underlying run, its structure and its convergence diagnostics are in the Solver tab.

What is in the file

The pickle is a single dictionary mapping an information-set key to a regret node. Nothing else — no game engine, no iteration counter, no exploitability trace. Each node carries two vectors the length of the legal action list at that node:

key = ( me_influence, opp_influence, me_coins, opp_coins,
       my_hand, revealed, phase, pending_action, pending_block )
node = { regret_sum: [float], strategy_sum: [float] }

Every slot is written from the perspective of whoever is to move at that node — in a response node the “me” fields belong to the responder, not the player who declared. That is what makes the same table usable by both seats.

The revealed slot pools both players' dead cards into one multiset — the solver does not track who revealed what. That is a deliberate abstraction: it keeps the tree small and it is the same information a card-counting human uses. It also means the solver cannot form reads about a specific opponent's history, only about how many copies remain.

Abstraction and its limits

  • Two players only. Every three-plus-player effect — who challenges first, the shared benefit of removing someone else's influence — is outside the model.
  • Exchange is collapsed. The Ambassador's draw-two-keep-two is treated as a claim with an outcome, not as a decision node over which cards to keep.
  • Which card you reveal on a loss is not a decision. It is folded into the state transition.
  • Coins cap at 12. Above ten you must coup, so the tail is unreachable in practice.
  • No opponent modelling. Equilibrium play, not exploitative play — it is unexploitable, not maximally profitable against a bad player.

Where the tree lives

Information sets by decision phase, with the share of equilibrium reach probability each phase carries. Response nodes outnumber action nodes almost four to one — most of Coup is deciding whether to believe someone.

Convergence

There is no exploitability number in the file, so convergence has to be inferred. Three proxies, all pointing the same way — the high-traffic core of the tree is settled, the tail is not:

Policy sharpness

How decisive the average strategy is at each information set. A converged Coup solution should be genuinely mixed in the middle of the tree and pure at the edges — which is what this distribution shows.

Coverage

Reach probability is extremely concentrated. Half of all equilibrium play happens in 4% of the information sets; the 49% of nodes with the least traffic carry 1% of it between them.

Read the tail with suspicion. 12.6% of information sets were visited fewer than 100 times. Their averaged strategies are close to arbitrary — visible in the opening chart below, where a couple of rare hands produce frequencies that clearly have not settled. Aggregates over many nodes are trustworthy; any single rare node is not.

Method

The action and card enumerations are not stored in the pickle — only integer tags. They were recovered from the structure itself, which is worth recording because it is the part that could silently go wrong:

  • Cards from block behaviour: the response option that spikes when a given card is in hand identifies the card. Holding tag 4 raises the third option against pending action 4 from 37% to 62% — tag 4 is the Contessa, and that option is the Contessa block. The same test fixes Duke, Captain and Ambassador; the Assassin falls out by elimination and is confirmed by the card-removal effect on challenge rate.
  • Actions from arity: the pending action with four responses can only be Steal, the one with three is Assassinate, and the two that admit a Duke block or no block at all separate Foreign Aid from Tax and Exchange.
  • Legal-list order from the same conditional spikes at action nodes, which is how Assassinate turns out to sit after Exchange rather than in enum position.
  • Averaging is strategy_sum normalised over positive entries — the CFR average strategy, which is the object that converges, not the current regret-matching policy. Aggregates weight each information set by its accumulated reach mass.

All frequencies on this tab and on the GTO Strategy tab are computed from the file by this method; nothing is hand-authored. tools/analyze_cfr.py reproduces the embedded blob byte for byte — it re-derives the encodings, fails loudly if the self-check does not hold, and prints the same JSON.
